A supplier offer arrives as a PDF with a letterhead, a product, a volume and a payment instrument. It looks complete. Most offers that later fall apart could have been read correctly on the first day.

## Who is signing

Start with the company, not the product. Is the seller a registered entity that can be found in its own registry? Does the signatory have authority to bind it? A seller who cannot show ownership and authority is not a seller yet.

## What is actually for sale

An offer names a product and a specification. Ask what proves the seller controls the goods: a warehouse receipt, a production allocation, a contract with the producer. Check the origin against sanctions rules before anything else.

## Whether the terms can be met

The Incoterms basis and the payment instrument have to fit together. A letter of credit under UCP 600 pays against documents: if the document route in the offer cannot produce those documents, the deal stalls at the bank, not at the port.

## What to check

- The seller's registration and the signatory's authority
- Evidence that the seller controls the goods, and their origin
- The Incoterms 2020 basis and who carries which risk
- The payment instrument and the documents it requires
- Sanctions screening of the companies, owners and vessels involved

If any of these is missing, the answer to the offer is a list of questions, not a signature.
